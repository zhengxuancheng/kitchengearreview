## What to Look For in a Digital Kitchen Scale

### Accuracy and Precision

The best kitchen scales measure in 1-gram increments. For coffee brewing or baking, look for 0.1-gram precision, which matters when you're measuring yeast, salt, or espresso beans. Most home scales drift by 1-2 grams over time, so a calibration feature or a test weight is a bonus — you can verify accuracy with a nickel (it weighs exactly 5 grams).

### Weight Capacity

Standard kitchen scales top out around 5 kilograms (11 pounds), which is fine for most home use — a chicken, a batch of dough, a mixing bowl of ingredients. For bread bakers who mix 2 kilos of dough at a time, 5 kg is the minimum. Heavy-duty scales go to 10 kg, useful if you're mixing large batches or weighing whole roasts.

### Display Readability

A backlit LCD makes a real difference when you're measuring ingredients at 6 PM with overhead kitchen lights competing. Some scales have front-facing displays that get blocked by large bowls. Look for displays on the scale body itself, or better yet, a removable display (rare at lower price points). The font size matters too — small gray numerals on a gray background are hard to read from standing height.

### Units and Modes

Most digital scales measure in grams, ounces, pounds, milliliters, and fluid ounces. The milliliter/fluid ounce conversion assumes water density, so it's only accurate for water and milk — don't rely on it for oil or honey. A tare/zero function is standard, but check whether it zeroes quickly. Some scales have a slow tare that makes you wait between ingredients, which gets annoying when you're adding six things to one bowl.

## FAQ

### Do I need 0.1-gram precision or is 1 gram enough?

For most cooking and baking, 1-gram precision is enough. The difference between 100 grams and 100.5 grams of flour doesn't matter for most recipes. You need 0.1-gram precision for yeast (recipes call for 2.5 or 7 grams), salt in small batches, baking soda and powder, coffee dose, and any recipe measured in single grams. If you bake bread or weigh coffee daily, get a 0.1-gram scale.

### Why would a baker need a scale instead of measuring cups?

Flour volume is wildly inconsistent. A cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 120 to 150 grams depending on scooping method, humidity, and how settled the flour is. That's a 20% variation. A scale eliminates the guesswork and means you can reproduce a good bake. It also makes following recipes from outside the US much easier, since most of the world bakes by weight.

### How do I calibrate a kitchen scale?

Most scales have a calibration mode: turn it on, press and hold the tare button for 3-5 seconds until "CAL" appears, then place the calibration weight on the platform. If you don't have a calibration weight, a US nickel weighs exactly 5 grams. Place it on the scale — if the reading is between 4.9 and 5.1 grams, the scale is accurate enough. Scales without calibration mode should be tested against a known weight and replaced if they drift more than 2 grams.

### Can I use a kitchen scale for portion control and meal prep?

Yes. A digital scale is the most honest tool for portion control. Scooping "one serving" of cereal, pasta, or nuts by eye underestimates by 30-50%. A scale gives you reliable portions. The Etekcity scale with Bluetooth nutrition tracking is especially useful here — weigh each ingredient, log it in the app, and it tallies your macros across the meal.

### What's the tare function and how do I use it?

Tare resets the scale to zero with a container on it. Put your bowl on the scale, press tare, add flour to 500 grams, press tare, add sugar to 200 grams, and so on. One bowl, multiple ingredients, no mental math. A slow tare is frustrating — you should be able to press it once and get instant zero.
