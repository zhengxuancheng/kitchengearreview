The difference between homemade pizza and pizzeria pizza is not the ingredients or the chef. It is the heat. Pizzerias use brick ovens that hit 800-900 degrees Fahrenheit, with a porous surface that pulls moisture from the dough and leaves char on the outside and chew on the inside.

Your home oven maxes out around 500 degrees. A stone or steel makes up for that by soaking up heat during a 45-minute preheat, then dumping it into the dough the moment the pizza lands. A bare baking sheet cannot do this. You end up with leopard spotting, oven spring, and a crust that actually tastes like it came from an oven.

We tested six pizza stones and baking steels across different materials and price points.

## What to Look For in a Pizza Stone or Baking Steel

Three materials dominate, each with different trade-offs. **Cordierite** (FibraMent, Old Stone Ovens) is a thermally stable ceramic composite that handles thermal shock better than basic ceramic. It heats evenly and rarely cracks, but takes longer to preheat and cools faster than steel. **Standard ceramic** (Pampered Chef, generic) is cheaper and retains heat reasonably well but cracks when exposed to sudden temperature changes — never put a frozen pizza on a hot ceramic stone. **Baking steel** (NerdChef, Baking Steel) is a slab of A36 steel that conducts heat more efficiently than stone. It preheats faster and transfers more energy into the dough, producing a better crust in a standard home oven. Steel does not crack, it can go under a broiler for Neapolitan-style char, and it doubles as a griddle for other cooking.

### Thickness and Weight

Thicker slabs store more thermal energy. Stones in the 1/2-inch to 3/4-inch range are standard; thinner stones cannot recover their heat between pizzas. Thicker is better, up to about 1 inch for stones and 1/4-inch to 1/2-inch for steel. Beyond those thicknesses, the preheat time becomes prohibitive and the weight makes handling dangerous. Speaking of weight: a 1/4-inch baking steel weighs about 10-12 pounds; a 1/2-inch steel weighs 20-25 pounds. Make sure you can safely get your stone or steel in and out of the oven without dropping it.

### Size and Oven Fit

Measure your oven interior before buying. Most standard ovens fit 14x16-inch stones or steels, but smaller ovens (24 inches wide or less) may only fit 12x14-inch surfaces. A larger surface gives you more room to slide pizzas on and off and accommodates larger pies. Round stones work well for pizzas but limit versatility — a rectangular stone doubles as a baking surface for breads, cookies, and flatbreads.

### Preheat Time

Stones and steels are not set-and-forget. A stone needs 30-45 minutes at 500 degrees to fully absorb heat. Steel needs 30-45 minutes. Do not rush the preheat — putting a pizza on a cold surface results in a soggy bottom. Some manufacturers claim shorter preheat times, but in practice, 45 minutes at maximum oven temperature is the right baseline for both materials.

## FAQ

### Does a pizza stone actually make a difference?

It makes a massive difference for crust quality. A pizza stone or steel stores thermal energy and transfers it into the dough rapidly, creating steam inside the dough that causes oven spring (the rapid rise of the crust edges). A standard baking sheet reflects heat and does not absorb moisture from the dough bottom, so the crust bakes more slowly and ends up dense and bread-like. The difference is immediately visible: a stone-baked crust has bubbles, char spots, and a chewy interior. A sheet-pan crust is flat and uniform.

### Baking steel vs. stone — which should I choose?

Steel produces a better crust in a standard home oven because it conducts heat more efficiently. The practical difference is visible: better leopard spotting, faster oven spring, and darker bottom char. Steel is also indestructible and doubles as a griddle. Stone is cheaper, lighter, and does not require seasoning or rust prevention. If you bake pizza once a month, get a stone. If you bake pizza weekly or want the best possible results, get a steel.

### Can I put my pizza stone directly on the oven rack?

Yes. Place the stone on the lowest oven rack to maximize heat exposure from the bottom heating element. Do not put it on the floor of the oven unless the manufacturer specifically says it is safe — the direct contact with the flame or element can cause thermal shock and cracking. Most stones should be placed on a rack position that leaves at least one inch of air circulation around the edges.

### How do I clean a pizza stone?

Never use soap. Stones are porous and absorb soap, which will leach into your pizza the next time you use it. Scrape off burned-on residue with a bench scraper or spatula while the stone is still warm. For stubborn spots, make a paste of baking soda and water, scrub gently, and rinse with clean water. Let the stone dry completely in the oven on low heat (200 degrees) before storing. Baking steel: wash with hot water and a stiff brush, dry immediately, and rub with a thin layer of vegetable oil to prevent rust.

### Why does my pizza stick to the stone?

Three common causes. One: the stone was not hot enough (preheat for the full 45 minutes). Two: the dough was too wet or the pizza sat on the peel too long before launching. Three: the peel was not floured or cornmealed adequately. Use a generous dusting of cornmeal or semolina on the peel before stretching the dough, and launch the pizza within 30 seconds of dressing it. If your dough has high hydration (above 70%), par-bake the crust for 2 minutes before adding toppings.

### Can I use a pizza stone on the grill?

Yes, if the stone is rated for high heat. Cordierite and FibraMent stones handle grill temperatures well. Place the stone on the grill grates, preheat with the grill lid closed for 15-20 minutes, then bake the pizza with the lid down. Avoid ceramic stones — the direct flame exposure and lid-closed temperatures can exceed their thermal shock threshold. Steel is safe on the grill and works brilliantly.

### How long should I preheat my pizza stone?

45 minutes at the highest oven setting (usually 500-550 degrees). Do not put the pizza in before the stone is fully heated. A cold stone produces a soggy crust and the pizza will stick. Set a timer and do not rush this step — it is the single most common mistake home pizza makers make.
