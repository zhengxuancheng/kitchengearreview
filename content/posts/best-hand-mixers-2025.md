A hand mixer is one of those kitchen tools you don't think about until you need it, and then you need it badly. Creaming butter and sugar for cookies. Whipping cream for a pie. Mashing potatoes for a holiday dinner. Beating eggs for a quiche. A stand mixer does these things better — but a stand mixer takes up counter space, costs serious money, and is overkill for most weekday cooking. A hand mixer fits in a drawer, costs $20-$100, and handles 80% of mixing tasks without drama.

I tested 7 hand mixers in my kitchen over a month. I made cookies, cakes, meringues, mashed potatoes, whipped cream, pancake batter, and frosting. I tested with thick cookie dough (the real test — if a hand mixer can handle stiff chocolate chip cookie dough, it can handle anything). I timed how long each took to whip cream and beat egg whites. Here are the ones that earned their place in the drawer.

## What to Look for in a Hand Mixer

### Motor Power

Motor power is measured in watts, and for hand mixers, more is generally better. A 200-250W motor handles light tasks (eggs, pancake batter, whipped cream) without struggling. A 300-400W motor is comfortable with cookie dough, mashed potatoes, and cake batter. Above 400W, you get into industrial territory — these mixers will power through stiff dough but they're heavier and louder.

The trick is that wattage isn't the whole story. Some manufacturers rate the motor output; others rate the power consumption. A 300W input motor might deliver 200W of actual mixing torque. Look for mixers with metal gears (not plastic) for heavy dough tasks — plastic gears strip under sustained load, especially when you push the beaters into thick cookie dough.

### Speed Settings and Mixing Action

Most hand mixers offer 5 to 9 speeds. More speeds give you finer control — a very low speed (1-2) prevents flour clouds when you start mixing dry ingredients, while high speeds (7-9) are for whipping air into cream and egg whites. The most useful range is 3-5 for everyday creaming and mixing.

The beaters themselves matter more than you'd think. Standard wire beaters are fine for most tasks. The best hand mixers use beater designs that scrape the bowl sides as they rotate, reducing the need to stop and scrape. Some premium models come with specialty attachments: dough hooks, whisk attachments, and blending wands.

### Weight and Ergonomics

A hand mixer that weighs 3+ pounds is fatiguing to hold through a batch of cookie dough. Lightweight models (1.5-2 lbs) are easier on the wrist but often use weaker motors and plastic gears. The sweet spot is 2.0-2.5 pounds with a comfortable grip and a trigger or thumb-button for speed control that doesn't require repositioning your hand.

## FAQ

### Can I use a hand mixer instead of a stand mixer?

For most tasks, yes. Hand mixers handle cookie dough, cake batter, frosting, whipped cream, and mashed potatoes as well as a stand mixer. The main things a stand mixer does better: (1) extended kneading of bread dough (hand mixer dough hooks overheat after 5-8 minutes), (2) heavy batches (double batches of cookie dough strain hand mixers), and (3) hands-free operation (you hold a hand mixer the whole time). If you bake bread weekly, get a stand mixer. If you bake cookies and cakes, a good hand mixer is enough.

### How do I clean a hand mixer?

Unplug it first. Wipe the body with a damp cloth — never submerge the body in water. Remove the beaters and wash them in warm soapy water or the dishwasher (check manufacturer instructions — some have dishwasher-safe beaters, others recommend hand washing). Dry beaters thoroughly before storing. For the mixer body, pay attention to the vent slots — batter splatter gets in there and bakes on. A damp toothpick or cotton swab cleans the vents.

### Why does my hand mixer smell like burning plastic?

A burning smell during use usually means one of three things: (1) the motor is overloaded — you're mixing dough that's too stiff for your mixer's power rating, (2) the vents are blocked by batter or dust, causing the motor to overheat, or (3) the brushes in the motor are wearing out (on older mixers). Stop immediately, unplug, and let it cool. If the smell persists on the next use with easier mixing, the motor may be damaged.

### What's the difference between dough hooks and beaters?

Dough hooks are spiral-shaped attachments designed for yeast dough (bread, pizza, rolls). They knead by stretching and folding the dough rather than cutting through it. Standard beaters cut through dough, which would overwork a yeasted dough and make it tough. If you make bread even occasionally, get a mixer that includes dough hooks. For cookie dough, cake batter, and frostings, standard beaters are the right choice.

### Do I need a 9-speed mixer or is 5 enough?

Five speeds is enough for most home baking. The standard speed progression (stir, cream, beat, whip) maps well to a 5-speed dial. Nine speeds give you finer control at the low end (a "fold" speed that won't spray flour, a "stir" speed gentler than speed 2) and more precision at the high end. The extra speeds matter most for delicate tasks like folding egg whites or making meringue. For cookies, cakes, and pancakes, 5 speeds are perfectly sufficient.
