An immersion blender — also called a stick blender or hand blender — is the tool that keeps you from lugging hot soup to the countertop blender one ladleful at a time. Instead of dirtying a full blender jar for a single smoothie or whisking vinaigrette by hand, you bring the blades to the food. And in 2025, these things do a lot more than purée: they chop, whisk, froth, and even mash potatoes.

The category has come a long way. Today's top models pack serious motors (500 to 1000-plus watts), variable speed triggers, comfortable grips, and attachment sets that can replace a small food processor. We tested six of the most popular — from the Breville Control Grip down to the sub-$35 Mueller — to figure out which ones earn their drawer space.

## What to Look For in an Immersion Blender

Before diving into our top picks, here are the factors that determine whether an immersion blender becomes your most-used tool or a dust collector.

### Motor Power and Wattage

Immersion blender motors range from about 250 watts in budget models to 1000+ watts in premium units. More wattage translates directly to blending power: a **300–500 watt** motor handles soft soups, smoothies with fresh fruit, and light emulsifying. A **500–700 watt** motor tackles frozen fruit, fibrous vegetables, and crushed ice with confidence. **800+ watt** motors power through tough ingredients — frozen bananas, raw beets, nuts — without bogging down or overheating. For daily soup makers and smoothie enthusiasts, 500+ watts is the sweet spot. For serious cooks who blend thick purées and frozen ingredients regularly, 800+ watts makes a noticeable difference in speed and smoothness.

### Speed Settings: Variable vs. Preset

Immersion blenders come with two main control styles: **variable speed dials** that let you fine-tune blending from gentle stir to full-power purée, and **preset speeds** (usually 2–3 settings plus turbo). Variable speed provides better control — you can start slow to avoid splattering hot soup, then ramp up to smooth. Preset speeds are simpler and work fine for most tasks, but lack the nuance that makes a great immersion blender feel responsive. Some premium models combine both: a variable dial with a turbo boost button for instant full power. For home cooks who care about control, variable speed is worth the premium.

### Attachments and Versatility

The best immersion blenders come with more than just the blending shaft. Common attachments include a **whisk** (for whipped cream, egg whites, emulsifying), a **chopper bowl** (mini food processor for onions, nuts, herbs), and a **milk frother** (for lattes and cappuccinos). Premium models add a **masher attachment** (potatoes, root vegetables) and a **blending beaker** (tall, narrow container that minimizes splashing). More attachments mean more versatility, but also more to store. Consider which attachments you'll actually use — a whisk and chopper bowl cover 90% of needs for most cooks.

### Comfort and Ergonomics

This is the most underrated factor. You'll hold your immersion blender for minutes at a time over a steaming pot — grip comfort, weight, and button placement matter profoundly. Look for a **rubberized, non-slip grip** that feels secure even with wet hands. The **trigger or button placement** should be reachable without repositioning your hand. **Weight** matters: lighter models (1.5–2 lbs) are easier to maneuver but may vibrate more, while heavier models (3+ lbs) feel more stable but cause fatigue during long blending sessions. The shaft length should reach the bottom of your tallest pot without your hand getting near the liquid — 8–10 inches is standard and works for most cookware.

### Ease of Cleaning

The blending shaft should detach from the motor body with a simple twist or button release. Stainless steel shafts are dishwasher-safe and resist staining from tomato sauce and turmeric. Blades should be accessible for rinsing — models with fixed blade guards that trap food are frustrating to clean. The motor body is never submersible, so a smooth surface that wipes clean easily is important — avoid models with deep crevices around buttons where food splatter accumulates.

## FAQ

### Can an immersion blender replace a full-size countertop blender?

Yes and no. For **soups, sauces, smoothies, baby food, and emulsifying (mayo, vinaigrette)**, an immersion blender does the job with less cleanup — no transferring hot liquids, no washing a heavy pitcher. For **crushing large volumes of ice, making nut butter, grinding grains, and ultra-smooth frozen drinks**, a full-size blender is still superior due to its sealed jar and more powerful motor. Many kitchens benefit from having both: an immersion blender for everyday cooking tasks and a full-size blender for heavy-duty blending. If you can only have one, the immersion blender is more versatile for cooking, while the countertop blender is better for smoothies and frozen drinks.

### How many watts do I really need?

For **occasional use** (weekly soup or sauce), 250–350 watts is adequate — the Braun MultiQuick 5 or Cuisinart Smart Stick handle these tasks easily. For **regular use** with frozen fruit, fibrous vegetables, and ice, 500+ watts makes a tangible difference — the Mueller Ultra-Stick and Vitamix power through tough ingredients without bogging down. For **daily heavy use**, 500+ watts with a quality motor (copper, not aluminum) ensures longevity and consistent performance. Wattage isn't everything — blade design and shaft geometry matter — but it's the best single indicator of blending capability.

### What's the best way to avoid splattering hot soup?

Start with the blade fully submerged in the liquid, then start on the lowest speed. Keep the blade guard flat against the bottom of the pot — lifting it even slightly creates a spray of hot liquid. If your blender has a variable speed dial, start at 1–2 and gradually increase. If it has preset speeds, pulse on the lowest setting until the vortex forms, then increase speed. Blending in a deep, narrow pot (or using the included blending beaker for smaller batches) also minimizes splatter. Never lift the blender out of the liquid while it's running — that's the #1 cause of soup on your ceiling.

### Are cordless immersion blenders worth it?

Cordless immersion blenders offer freedom of movement and are convenient for quick tasks — no finding an outlet, no cord getting in the way. However, they have two significant trade-offs: **battery life** (10–15 minutes of continuous blending, after which you're waiting 2–3 hours to recharge) and **power** (most cordless models max out around 250 watts due to battery constraints). If you use your blender for occasional 30-second tasks, cordless is fine. If you blend soups for 5+ minutes or tackle frozen ingredients regularly, corded is still the better choice. We stuck to corded models in this roundup for maximum power and reliability.

### How do I clean an immersion blender properly?

The easiest method: fill a tall container or the blending beaker with warm water and a drop of dish soap, submerge the blending shaft, and run the blender for 10–15 seconds. Rinse under running water, and it's clean. For stubborn residue (nut butter, sticky sauces), detach the shaft and wash by hand with a brush. Most stainless steel shafts are dishwasher-safe (top rack), but hand-washing takes 30 seconds and extends the life of the blade seal. Never submerge the motor body in water — wipe it with a damp cloth. For the whisk and chopper attachments, dismantle and wash components separately to prevent food buildup at connection points.
