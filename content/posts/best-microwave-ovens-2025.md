A microwave is one of those appliances most people use every single day but barely think about. You grab whatever was on sale at the big-box store and live with uneven heating and a confusing interface for years. A decent microwave — one that fits how you actually cook — makes a real difference. These days you can get smart models that scan barcodes, inverter ones that defrost without cooking the edges, or basic workhorses that just heat reliably. We tested 7 to find the ones worth your counter space.

## How We Evaluated

Each microwave was scored on:

- **Heating evenness** — does the center get hot while the edges stay cold, or does it heat evenly across the turntable?
- **Wattage and speed** — higher wattage means faster cooking, but raw power doesn't always mean even results
- **Sensor cooking** — does the humidity sensor actually stop at the right time, or does it overcook everything?
- **Interface and usability** — knob vs. button, presets that make sense, and whether you need to read a manual to defrost a bagel
- **Capacity and footprint** — interior space vs. counter space consumed
- **Extra features** — inverter technology, smart features, air fry mode, broil element

## FAQ

### Is a higher wattage microwave always better?

Higher wattage means faster cooking, but it doesn't automatically mean even cooking. A 1250W microwave with poor waveguide design can leave cold spots just as badly as a 700W model. What matters more than raw wattage is how the power is delivered. **Inverter** microwaves (Panasonic) deliver consistent power at any level, so low-power defrost is genuinely low power. Conventional microwaves cycle on and off, so "30% power" means full blast for 3 seconds, off for 7 seconds — which leads to uneven results, especially with frozen food. For most people, 1000-1200W with decent sensor cooking is the sweet spot. Below 900W, cooking times get noticeably long, and you may struggle to heat dense foods like leftovers evenly.

### What is sensor cooking and do I need it?

Sensor cooking uses a humidity sensor inside the microwave cavity to detect when food is done. As food heats, it releases steam — the sensor measures the humidity level and automatically stops the microwave when it detects the steam pattern associated with fully cooked food. Good sensor cooking (Panasonic, Breville, Samsung) is genuinely useful for things like frozen dinners, vegetables, and leftovers — you don't need to guess the time, and the microwave won't overcook. Cheap sensor cooking (on budget models) is inconsistent and often stops too early or too late. If you frequently microwave frozen foods or reheat leftovers, sensor cooking is worth paying for. If you mostly make popcorn and heat coffee, it's unnecessary.

### Can I put metal in a microwave?

Never put metal in a microwave unless the manufacturer explicitly states it's safe. Metal reflects microwaves instead of absorbing them, which can cause arcing (sparks), damage the interior, and in extreme cases, start a fire. This includes aluminum foil, metal-trimmed plates, twist ties, and takeout containers with metal handles. The exceptions: some modern microwaves have stainless steel interiors (the walls are fine, the metal is grounded) and some microwaves specify that small amounts of smooth, flat aluminum foil are safe to shield food edges during defrosting. When in doubt, use microwave-safe glass or ceramic cookware.

### How do I clean a microwave without scrubbing?

Fill a microwave-safe bowl with 1 cup of water and 2 tablespoons of vinegar or lemon juice. Microwave on high for 3-5 minutes until the door is steamy. Leave the door closed for another 5 minutes to let the steam loosen baked-on food. Open carefully (steam is hot), remove the bowl, and wipe the interior with a sponge. Everything — even dried sauce and grease — will come off without scrubbing. For stubborn spots, dip the sponge in the vinegar water. Clean the turntable and roller ring separately in the dishwasher or with soap and water.

### Is a flat-floor microwave better than one with a turntable?

Flat-floor microwaves (Samsung, Sharp) maximize usable interior space because there's no turntable mechanism taking up height. You can fit taller containers, rectangular casserole dishes, and multiple plates side by side. The trade-off is that flat-floor designs use a rotating antenna or stirrer fan to distribute waves, which is less effective than a turntable at eliminating cold spots. Food near the corners of a flat-floor microwave often heats less evenly than food on a turntable. The Samsung Bespoke's Triple Distribution System mitigates this, but turntable-based microwaves still win on evenness. For most people, a turntable is better. For anyone who regularly heats tall containers or oddly-shaped dishes, a flat floor is worth the compromise.

### How much counter space does a microwave actually need?

A microwave's listed width and depth are usually the minimum — you also need 3-4 inches of clearance on each side and above for ventilation. A typical 1.2 cu ft microwave (like the Toshiba EM131A5C) is about 19 inches wide, 16 inches deep, and 12 inches tall. Add clearance and you need roughly 24" x 20" of counter space. The large 2.2 cu ft models (Panasonic, Sharp) need 28" x 22" or more. Before buying, measure your countertop and confirm the microwave fits with the recommended clearance. If counter space is tight, the Amazon Basics or GE JES1109RRSS have the smallest footprints.
