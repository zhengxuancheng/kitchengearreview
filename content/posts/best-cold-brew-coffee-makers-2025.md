I drink iced coffee all year, not just in summer. But iced coffee made from hot-brewed coffee that sat in the fridge always tastes a little sad: a little bitter, a little flat, like the coffee equivalent of reheated pizza. Cold brew fixes that. Steeping coarse grounds in cold water for 12 to 24 hours pulls out the sweet, chocolatey flavors and leaves most of the acid behind. The result is a concentrate smooth enough to drink black and strong enough to survive a glass of milk.

I tested six cold brew makers over a few weeks: classic pitchers, glass towers, a model with a dispensing tap, and a big-batch jug. I brewed the same beans in all of them, timed the steeps, measured how much concentrate each one actually produced, and cleaned each one the way a normal person would on a busy morning. Here's what I found.

## What to Look For in a Cold Brew Maker

### Concentrate or Ready to Drink

Cold brew makers split into two camps. Concentrate brewers make a strong extract you dilute with water or milk, usually at a 1:1 or 2:1 ratio. One batch becomes four or five drinks, which is why most makers are concentrates. Ready-to-drink brewers like the Hario Mizudashi make a weaker brew you can pour straight over ice. Both work. The concentrate route is more economical and stores better; the ready-to-drink route is simpler in the morning when you haven't had coffee yet.

### Capacity

Think about how many drinks you need per batch. A 1-quart brewer makes roughly four to five drinks. A 1-liter maker does the same. A 1.5-liter or 2-quart system stretches to a week's worth for a household, or a solid couple of days if two people drink it. The big 64-ounce jugs are for people who entertain or go through a batch in three days. Buy for your actual consumption, not the size that looks impressive on the counter.

### Filter Quality

The filter is the whole game. Cold brew grounds are fine, and a cheap mesh filter lets silt through, leaving you with a cloudy cup and grit at the bottom. The best makers use a fine stainless mesh, sometimes double layered, that catches everything. If the filter is removable, it should also be easy to rinse, because grounds packed into a mesh are miserable to clean once they've dried.

### Ease of Cleaning

Every cold brew maker has a steeping period of 12 to 24 hours, so the thing you'll actually deal with daily is cleanup. Pitchers with wide mouths and removable filters win. Narrow-necked glass towers need a bottle brush and some patience. Models with dispensing taps add a part that can get gunk in it if you don't flush it. Ask yourself how you clean dishes at 7am on a Tuesday, and buy accordingly.

### Fridge Footprint

Cold brew spends its whole life in the refrigerator, so measure your fridge shelf before you buy. Tall glass towers look great but need vertical space. Wide pitchers fit on a standard shelf. The 64-ounce jugs are tall and bulky. I'd also check that the lid seals properly, because a leaking cold brew maker is a sticky disaster waiting in the crisper drawer.

## FAQ

### What's the difference between cold brew and iced coffee?

Iced coffee is hot-brewed coffee that's been chilled, usually by pouring it over ice. Cold brew is coffee that's steeped in cold water for 12 to 24 hours and never heated. Cold brew comes out smoother and less acidic because the cold water extracts different compounds than heat does. If hot coffee bothers your stomach, cold brew is worth trying.

### What coffee-to-water ratio should I use?

For a concentrate, start with 1 part coffee to 4 parts water by weight. That means about 1 cup of coarsely ground coffee per 4 cups of water. For ready-to-drink strength, use 1 part coffee to 8 parts water. Grind coarse, about the texture of breadcrumbs. Too fine a grind and the brew turns bitter and cloudy.

### How long should cold brew steep?

Most makers recommend 12 to 24 hours at room temperature or in the fridge. Twelve hours gives a milder cup, 24 hours a stronger one. Beyond 24 hours, the brew starts to pick up bitter notes, so there's no benefit to leaving it longer. The beauty of cold brew is the window is forgiving; anywhere in that range makes a drinkable cup.

### Is cold brew less acidic than hot coffee?

Yes, noticeably. The cold steeping process extracts about 70 percent less acid than hot brewing, which is why cold brew is smoother and easier on sensitive stomachs. It's not acid-free, so people with severe reflux should still go easy, but most people find it a real improvement.

### How long does cold brew last in the fridge?

A concentrate stored in a sealed container lasts up to two weeks in the fridge. Ready-to-drink cold brew is best within five to seven days. If it starts tasting flat or sour, it's past its prime. Brew smaller batches more often if you find yourself throwing coffee away.
