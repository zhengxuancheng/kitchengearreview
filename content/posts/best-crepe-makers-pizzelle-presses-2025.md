Crepes look fancy but they are not hard to make, provided you have the right surface. A dedicated electric crepe maker heats evenly and gives you control over thin, even batter distribution — which a regular skillet cannot match no matter how much butter you add. Pizzelle presses are even simpler: pour batter, close the lid, wait a minute, and you get Italian wafer cookies without any rolling or shaping.

I spent a few weeks testing six crepe makers and pizzelle presses across different styles. Some produced paper-thin crepes on the first try. Others took practice. A couple of them I would not use again.

## What to Look For in a Crepe Maker or Pizzelle Press

### Nonstick Surface Quality

The nonstick coating determines how easily your crepe releases and how long the surface lasts. PTFE-based coatings (Teflon) work well but scratch easily — you need silicone or wooden utensils only. Ceramic nonstick coatings are more scratch-resistant and contain no PTFE, but they lose their release properties faster over time. For either type, avoid metal spatulas and abrasive cleaning pads.

### Temperature Control

A good crepe maker has adjustable heat. Crepes need moderate heat to cook through without browning too fast — around 375°F works for thin batter. Pizzelle batter needs higher heat (around 400-425°F) to get the crisp texture and golden color. A single-temperature machine limits you to one type of food. Look for adjustable thermostats with clearly marked temperature scales.

### Surface Size

A 10-inch cooking surface makes standard crepes (about 8 inches) with room to spread. Smaller 8-inch surfaces work for appetizer-sized crepes but feel cramped for full breakfast crepes. Pizzelle presses typically have 4-5 inch round plates that produce one cookie at a time. Larger rectangular presses make two cookies at once.

### Immersion Ring vs. Spreader

Most electric crepe makers come with a batter immersion ring — a wooden or plastic frame that keeps batter contained while you spread it. You dip the ring in batter, pour it onto the surface, and spread outward with a spreading tool. This produces consistently thin, round crepes. Without it, your crepes will be uneven and oddly shaped.

### Indicator Light

A temperature-ready indicator light is essential. Without one you are guessing whether the surface is hot enough. Cold crepe batter on a not-quite-hot-enough surface produces thick, gummy crepes. Too hot and the batter sets before you can spread it.

## FAQ

### Can I use regular pancake batter in a crepe maker?

You can but it will not produce crepes. Pancake batter has leavening agents (baking powder or baking soda) that make it rise, producing thick and fluffy results instead of thin crepes. Use a proper crepe batter: flour, eggs, milk, melted butter, and a pinch of salt. No leavening.

### How do I clean a crepe maker?

Let the surface cool completely. Wipe with a damp paper towel. For stuck-on batter, use a soft sponge and mild soap — never abrasive cleaners or metal scrubbers. Nonstick surfaces scratch easily. Most manufacturers recommend avoiding submerging the appliance in water.

### What is the difference between a crepe maker and a regular pan?

A dedicated crepe maker heats more evenly across a flat surface and reaches consistent temperatures. Regular pans often have hot spots and curved sides that make batter spreading difficult. The immersion ring system on electric crepe makers produces consistently round, thin crepes that are harder to achieve with a pan.

### Can a pizzelle press make anything besides pizzelle?

Some people use pizzelle presses to make thin waffle cookies, cannoli shells (rolled while warm), or even thin hash brown patties. The CucinaPro press specifically should only be used for batter-based cookies. The VillaWare FlipSide handles both crepes and pizzelle because it has two separate surfaces.

### Why are my crepes sticking?

Either the surface is not hot enough or the batter has too little fat. Make sure the indicator light shows the surface is at temperature before pouring batter. Add melted butter to your batter — about two tablespoons per cup of flour. If the nonstick coating is old and degraded, it may be time for a replacement.
