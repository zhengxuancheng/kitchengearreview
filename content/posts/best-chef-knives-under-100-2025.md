You don't need to spend two hundred dollars on a chef's knife to cook well. The $70 knife in the right hands outperforms the $300 knife in the wrong ones — and there are excellent blades available under $100 if you know where to look. The hard part is filtering out the dozens of cheap stamped blades with terrible edge geometry that dull after one meal prep session.

We tested seven chef's knives under $100 across multiple styles: Western, Japanese-style gyuto, Chinese cleaver, and nakiri. Each was used for three weeks of daily cooking — dicing onions, slicing raw chicken, mincing herbs, and breaking down the occasional squash. Here's what we found.

## What to Look For in a Chef's Knife Under $100

### Blade Steel and Edge Retention

At this price point, you're looking at one of three steel types. **X50CrMoV15** or equivalently marked German stainless (used by Wusthof, Zwilling, Mercer) offers good corrosion resistance and a reasonable edge that's easy to sharpen but doesn't hold the razor edge as long as harder steels. **VG-10** (a Japanese stainless with vanadium) holds an edge significantly longer but is harder to sharpen and slightly more brittle. **AUS-8** and **AUS-10** sit between them — better edge retention than German steel, easier to sharpen than VG-10. Avoid mystery steel knives from no-name brands. If the product page doesn't specify the steel, the knife is not worth buying.

### Blade Geometry

How the blade is ground matters more than the steel type for how well it cuts. **Full-flat grind** (the blade tapers in a straight line from spine to edge) is common on German knives and slices well through dense ingredients. **Hollow or Granton edge** (divots along the blade face) reduces friction for thin slicing but makes the blade weaker at the edge. **Thin convex or concave grinds** are found on Japanese-style knives and pass through food with less resistance but are more fragile at the edge. The general rule: thinner blades cut better but chip easier. For a first chef's knife, a medium-thickness blade with a 15-degree edge angle is the sweet spot.

### Handle Comfort and Balance

A knife that's unbalanced or uncomfortable will annoy you every single time you use it, regardless of how sharp the blade is. Two main handle styles exist. **Full-tang handles** (the steel runs the full length of the handle, sandwiched between scales) offer better balance and durability. **Partial-tang or rat-tail tang** handles are lighter and cheaper but less durable. Handle material matters: polypropylene and ABS are durable and dishwasher-safe (though you shouldn't put good knives in the dishwasher anyway), wood feels better but requires maintenance, and composite materials like Fibrox offer the best grip when wet. The pinch grip (thumb and forefinger on the blade, remaining fingers wrapped around the handle) should feel natural — if the handle's bolster bumps your knuckles or the blade's heel is uncomfortable, the knife won't work for you.

### Blade Length

An 8-inch (200mm) chef's knife is the standard for a reason — it handles the widest range of tasks from dicing onions to slicing a roast chicken. A 6-inch is more maneuverable for smaller hands but struggles with larger ingredients. A 10-inch offers more slicing reach but feels unwieldy for detail work. If you're buying one knife, buy 8 inches.

### Rockwell Hardness (HRC)

The Rockwell hardness rating tells you how hard the steel is. For kitchen knives, the useful range is 54-62 HRC. Softer blades (54-57 HRC) are easier to sharpen, less brittle, and less likely to chip — but they dull faster. Harder blades (58-62 HRC) hold an edge much longer but are harder to sharpen and more prone to chipping if abused. Most German knives sit at 55-57 HRC. Japanese-style knives in this price range sit at 58-60 HRC. Harder isn't always better — for a daily driver that you'll hand-sharpen, 57-58 HRC is the ideal range.

## FAQ

### Is a \$50 chef's knife good enough for daily cooking?

Yes, if you choose the right one. A Victorinox Fibrox at \$45 will outperform many \$150 knives in actual kitchen use. The limiting factor isn't the price — it's edge maintenance. A \$50 knife that's sharpened regularly out-cuts a \$300 knife that's been abused in a dishwasher. Spend the money on a good sharpening stone (a \$30 King 1000/6000 combination stone is enough) rather than a more expensive knife.

### How often should I sharpen a chef's knife?

It depends on the steel and how much you use it. With the German stainless knives (Victorinox, Mercer, Wusthof), a quick honing on a steel rod before each use session keeps the edge aligned, and a proper sharpening on a stone every 2-4 weeks restores the edge. With harder Japanese steels (Tojiro, MAC), honing has minimal effect (the hard steel doesn't realign easily), but the edge lasts 3-6 weeks between stone sharpenings. The carbon steel CCK needs honing before each use and a stone every 1-2 weeks. The rule: the moment you feel the knife dragging rather than slicing through a tomato skin, it's time to sharpen.

### Should I buy a Western or Japanese chef's knife?

The difference is more practical than stylistic. Western knives (Victorinox, Mercer, Wusthof) have thicker blades, softer steel, and are more forgiving of abuse — they handle heavy chopping, bone contact, and occasional use on a glass cutting board (which you should not be using). Japanese knives (Tojiro, MAC, CCK) have thinner blades and harder steel — they cut better but require more careful handling, immediate cleaning, and proper cutting boards. If you're buying your first good knife and tend to be rough on kitchen equipment, start with a Western knife. If you already have basic knife skills and want better cutting performance, go Japanese.

### Can I put a chef's knife in the dishwasher?

No. Dishwasher detergent is abrasive and will dull the edge. The high heat and moisture can warp wooden handles and cause rust on carbon steel. The water jets banging the knife against other utensils chips the edge. And the drying cycle's heat can soften handle adhesives. Hand wash, dry immediately, store in a knife block or magnetic strip. Dishwasher-damaged knives lose their edge permanently — there is no recovery.

### What's the best way to store a chef's knife?

A magnetic strip on the wall or a knife block. Loose in a drawer damages both the blade and your fingers when you reach in. A blade guard works for storage but not for daily access. Magnetic strips are the most practical: the knife is visible, accessible, and the edge never touches anything. The one risk is that powerful magnets can slightly affect the steel's temper over very long periods (years), but this is not a practical concern for home cooks.

### Do I need a 10-inch chef's knife or is 8 inches enough?

Eight inches is the right length for almost everything. A 10-inch knife offers more slicing reach for large roasts or watermelons, but it's heavier and less maneuverable for detail work. If you regularly cook large cuts of meat or big squash, a 10-inch knife as a second blade makes sense. For most home cooks, an 8-inch chef's knife plus a 5-6 inch utility knife covers every cutting task. The exception is professional cooks who can handle the extra length without loss of control.
